Cat grooming

Reading the Cat: What the Groomer Is Watching While They Work

Cats give plenty of warning. Most people are looking at the wrong end.

Owners tend to watch a cat's face during grooming, and the face is the least informative part. A cat that is about to stop cooperating has usually been saying so for a full minute somewhere else on its body, and a groomer who can read that never gets to the point of a struggle.

The tail goes first. A slow swish is mild irritation and is fine; a fast flick, or a tail that starts thumping the mat, means the cat has had enough of whatever is happening right now. The ears rotate backwards and flatten slightly — not the dramatic pinned-back ears people picture, just a quarter turn. The skin along the back starts to twitch or ripple. The body goes still in a particular way that is nothing like relaxation; a settled cat is loose and a shut-down cat is rigid, and the difference matters enormously because the rigid cat is the one that explodes.

What our groomers do with that information is simple and it is the whole method: stop, take the hands off, wait five or ten seconds, and resume with something less objectionable. That pause, repeated throughout a session, is why a session finishes calmly. A groomer working through the signals to save two minutes will spend those two minutes later restraining a frightened animal, and the cat will remember it next time. In Koregaon Park, where a lot of the cats we groom are long-established and thoroughly used to getting their own way, reading the signals correctly is most of the job.

Pune seasons

Summer, Sun and Cats Who Live on the Terrace

KP has the gardens and the light. That comes with a few things to watch.

Koregaon Park has more usable outdoor space per cat than almost anywhere in Pune — terraces, verandahs, walled gardens with old trees. Cats use all of it, and they use it in April at two in the afternoon, because a cat's idea of a pleasant temperature is well above ours.

Two things are worth knowing. The first is that shaving a cat for summer is mostly a misunderstanding. A cat's coat insulates in both directions and also blocks sun; a clipped cat is not obviously cooler and is considerably more exposed. If your cat is genuinely struggling with heat, shade, water and airflow do far more than a clipper. What does help is removing the dead undercoat, which traps heat without providing any useful function — a proper deshedding session in March or April is the summer intervention that actually works.

The second is sun exposure on the thin-furred parts. Cats with white or pale ears, a pink nose or a sparsely furred belly can and do get sunburnt on a terrace, and repeated sunburn on the ear tips is a recognised route to skin cancer in cats — vets in India see it regularly in white cats who sunbathe. It is worth checking your cat's ear tips occasionally for redness, crusting or a rough patch that does not resolve. Our groomers look at ear tips as part of every session and will tell you if something looks worth showing a vet.

Frequently asked questions

Can you groom a cat who's used to being bathed professionally but in a salon setting?

Transitioning from salon to home grooming is usually smooth. Cats who are already grooming-experienced are typically fine with the session itself — and they often do better at home because the environment is familiar and the one-on-one attention is more consistent.

Do you groom cats with flat faces like Persians differently?

Yes. Flat-faced cats (Persians, Exotic Shorthairs, Himalayans) need careful eye area cleaning, gentle wiping of facial folds, and we avoid any product getting near the face during the bath. The drying around the face is also more careful since flat-faced cats can be more sensitive.

Is there a premium or speciality package for longhair breeds?

Our standard session covers everything a longhair cat needs. For show cats or cats with very extensive coat requirements, we can discuss a tailored extended session at the time of booking.

My cat is white and sunbathes on the terrace every afternoon. Should I be concerned?

Mildly, and it is worth knowing about rather than worrying about. White and pale-eared cats have very little pigment protecting the thin skin on the ear tips and nose, and repeated sun exposure there is a recognised risk factor for skin cancer in cats — it is one of the more common tumours vets see in white cats in sunny climates. Look at the ear tips every few weeks for persistent redness, crusting, or a rough patch that does not heal. Shade during the harshest hours is the simple fix. Our groomers check ear tips as part of every session and will flag anything that looks worth a vet's opinion.

We have a Persian and a Sphynx. Do they need the same schedule?

No, and they are close to opposite ends of the range. The Persian needs a session every four to six weeks and daily combing at home, because the coat mats faster than most people expect in Pune's humidity. The Sphynx needs bathing more often than any coated cat — roughly every two weeks — because there is no fur to absorb skin oils, so they build up on the surface and in the folds, and the nail beds and ears need cleaning at the same interval. Booking them on the same visit works fine; we just alternate which one gets the full treatment each time.

Can I book and pay for a session on someone else's behalf?

Yes, and it is a fairly common request here — usually a gift, or someone arranging grooming for a parent or a family member who is travelling. Book in the normal way but give us the cat's address and the contact number of whoever will actually be at home, and tell us who to coordinate with on the day. Payment can be settled by UPI from wherever you are rather than at the door. Just make sure the person at the flat knows we are coming; a groomer arriving unannounced to a surprised household is not the gift anyone intended.

The lanes here are narrow with no parking. Does that affect the visit?

Not at all. Groomers arrive by two-wheeler with the full kit in one shoulder bag — nothing is unloaded at your gate and no vehicle needs a bay. It is precisely why doorstep grooming works on the numbered lanes and the bungalow roads off North Main Road.

Our cat has the run of a large bungalow. Which room should the session happen in?

The smallest quiet room with a door that shuts and a tap nearby — usually a bathroom or a guest room. A large house feels like an advantage but it is the opposite for a cat session: more space means more places to bolt to and a longer settling period. A contained room is calmer for the cat and safer for everyone.

We have staff at home during the day. Can they manage the appointment?

Yes, if the cat is comfortable with them and you are contactable by phone. The limit is decisions: if the coat is matted enough that clipping down is the kinder option, that call comes to you rather than to whoever happens to be at home. We would rather hold the session five minutes than change your cat on someone else's authority.

My cat goes out into the garden. Does that change the grooming plan?

It changes what we inspect. An outdoor-access cat gets a full tick and flea check before anything else starts — between the toes, inside the ears, along the jawline and at the base of the tail. Garden soil also works deep into a dense undercoat and does not rinse out in a single pass, so the wash stage takes longer than for an indoor cat.

We are relocating abroad. What should be done before the flight?

A full groom two to three days ahead — coat fully combed out with no mats, nails short — so nothing needs doing on travel day. Do not bathe a cat the morning of a flight. Schedule tightly around the vet health certificate and loosely around the grooming; the certificate is the document with the hard deadline.
