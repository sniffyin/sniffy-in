At your doorstep

Why Cats Do So Much Better at Home Than at Salons

Territory matters more to cats than anything else.

Cats mark and defend their territory actively. It's not just preference — it's deeply wired behaviour. A salon is an alien territory full of other animals' smells, unfamiliar humans, and no safe retreat. A cat taken to a salon is dealing with all of that in addition to the grooming itself. At home, the territory is theirs. The smells are familiar. There are places to retreat to between steps. The result is consistently lower stress and better grooming outcomes — not because we're doing anything dramatically different, but because the environment makes it possible.

What we do

Ears, Eyes and Paw Pads — The Checks a Bath Skips

The three places problems start, and the three places nobody looks.

Most people picture grooming as washing and brushing. The parts that most often turn out to matter are none of those.

Ears first. A cat's ear canal bends, so you cannot see into it and neither can your cat clean it. Wax builds quietly, and dark crumbly wax in one ear rather than both is the classic sign of mites. We clean gently with a cat-specific solution and, crucially, we look before we clean — an ear that is already red and sore should go to a vet, not get flushed.

Eyes next. Flat-faced breeds have shortened tear ducts, so tears run onto the face instead of draining, and the constant damp under the eye breeds the reddish-brown staining you see on so many Persians. It is not dirt and scrubbing will not shift it; it needs wiping daily and keeping the fur short there.

And paw pads, which almost nobody checks. Fur growing long between the pads makes a cat skid on tiled floors — a real fall risk for a stiff older cat. Claws left untrimmed curve round into the pad itself, which is painful and easy to miss under the fur. We trim the tuft between the pads flush and check each claw individually. It takes three minutes and it is the change owners most often tell us they noticed at home.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose between a standard grooming session and a full groom with haircut?

Standard sessions cover bath, brush, nails, and ears — everything your cat needs for health and hygiene. Haircuts (lion cuts, teddy bear cuts) are aesthetic add-ons and are most useful for heavily matted longhair cats where coat management is becoming unsustainable. We can advise at the session whether a haircut would be beneficial.

My cat gets dandruff — can grooming help?

Often yes. Cat dandruff (dander flaking) is frequently caused by dry skin, poor diet, or insufficient grooming frequency. A conditioning wash with a moisturising shampoo, followed by proper drying, can significantly reduce visible dandruff. If it's persistent, it may have an underlying cause worth discussing with a vet.

Is the session noisy? My cat is sound-sensitive.

The main noise element is the hair dryer. We use a lower-noise dryer on the lowest speed setting and can do most of the drying with towels if your cat is very sensitive to sound. Mention sound sensitivity at booking and we'll plan around it.

My Persian's eyes water constantly and the fur underneath has gone reddish-brown. Can you fix that?

We can improve it, but it will come back, and it is worth understanding why. Flat-faced breeds have compressed tear ducts, so tears spill onto the face rather than draining away. The reddish-brown colour is pigment from the tears themselves oxidising in permanently damp fur — it is not dirt, and scrubbing harder does nothing. What helps is keeping the fur in the eye folds trimmed short so it dries between tears, and a gentle wipe every day at home. We will clean and trim the area at each session and show you the wiping technique. If one eye is worse than the other, or the discharge is thick rather than clear, that is a vet visit rather than a grooming issue.

Yes. Bibewadi gaon, the societies along Sinhagad Road, Sahakarnagar, Parvati, Katraj and Kondhwa are all within our regular route. It is a dense, well-connected pocket, which usually means we can offer same-week slots here even when the outer suburbs are booked out.

My old cat has stopped cleaning himself properly and has started to smell. Can grooming help?

Yes, and this is one of the cases where regular grooming makes the most difference to daily life. A cat that has stopped self-grooming is usually telling you something — arthritis that makes twisting painful, weight that puts his own back out of reach, dental pain that makes licking unpleasant, or simply age. The coat goes greasy and clumped, and the smell follows. A proper wash, a deshedding pass and a sanitary trim resets it, and a six-week rhythm keeps it managed. But do also mention it to your vet: a sudden stop in self-grooming is a recognised early sign of pain or illness, and it is worth ruling that out rather than treating it as ordinary ageing.

Our building is old with narrow stairs and no lift. Does that work?

Entirely — the groomer carries one shoulder bag and your cat never leaves the flat. That is the core advantage over a salon: no carrier, no stairs for the cat, no car journey. Older central-south buildings are a routine address for us.

Our cat is diabetic and on insulin. Can she still be groomed?

Usually yes, with a shorter session and careful timing. Tell us at booking, and schedule the slot so it does not clash with her injection and feeding routine — a missed or delayed meal matters more for a diabetic cat than the grooming does. We keep sessions brief, watch for any sign of distress, and stop early rather than finish a checklist.

Can I book just a nail trim for our senior cat?

Yes, and for an older cat it is often the sensible choice over a full session. Elderly cats stop wearing their claws down and the outer claws can curl into the pad, which is painful and completely preventable. A short maintenance visit for nails and an ear clean is available below full session price.

Is it a problem if children are around during the session?

It is the one thing worth managing beforehand. A cat under handling is already spending all its patience, and children coming in and out of the room is the usual reason a settling cat stops settling. We work in one closed room — please keep it closed, especially through the drying stage.
