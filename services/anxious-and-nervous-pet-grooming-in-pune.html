The short answer

Which pets need a gentler approach

Fear looks different in different animals. Knowing the signs is the first step to making grooming bearable.

Dogs that tremble, hide or freeze

Hiding behind furniture, tail tucked, whale eye and lip licking are fear, not stubbornness. They need space and time, not firmness.

Reactive or barking dogs

A dog that lunges at the dryer or the groomer is usually frightened and trying to make the scary thing go away. Distance and predictability reduce it.

Rescue and adopted animals

Unknown history means unknown triggers. Some have been hurt by grooming before, and a slow reset can undo a lot.

Cats that hiss, swat or hide

Cats react to being restrained far more strongly than dogs. A cat that is not forced is far less likely to panic or scratch.

Pets that panic at noise

Hairdryers, clippers and even running water can be frightening. The groomer introduces each sound separately and gradually.

Pets with a bad past grooming experience

A single rough salon visit can cause lasting fear. The cure is a series of calm, short, successful visits.

How we groom a nervous pet without force

There is no trick to it. It is patience, a plan and the willingness to stop. These steps are how a visit usually runs.

  1. 1. A briefing before the visit

    You tell us what frightens your pet and what has gone wrong before. The groomer plans around it and may suggest coming at a quiet time of day.

  2. 2. Arrive quietly and wait

    No loud greeting, no reaching towards the pet. The groomer sits low, avoids staring and lets your pet approach in its own time.

  3. 3. Introduce the tools one at a time

    Brush, towel, water, clippers and dryer are each shown, sniffed and rewarded before being used. A tool that is never introduced is a tool that is always a surprise.

  4. 4. Work in short, easy stages

    A few minutes at a time, starting with the least worrying parts, with breaks. Anything the pet will not accept is skipped or left for a later visit.

  5. 5. Read the body language, always

    Stiffening, lip licking, ears back, growling or a flicking tail mean slow down or stop. We do not push through the signs.

  6. 6. Finish on a good note and report

    End while the pet is still coping. You are told what was achieved, what was not and what to practise before next time.

Anxious & nervous pets: what to know before you book

Every life stage

Why home is the best place for a nervous pet

Remove the stress before the grooming starts

A frightened dog taken to a salon has to cope with the car ride, new smells, other dogs, noise, a strange room and a stranger, all before the first drop of water. By the time grooming begins, the stress bucket is already full.

At home, almost all of that disappears. Your pet is on familiar ground, with its own smells, its own bed and the people it trusts. The groomer is the only new element, and that can be introduced slowly.

For many nervous pets, this alone changes the outcome. A dog that shakes in a salon waiting room can be a perfectly cooperative bath subject in its own bathroom.

  • No car journey
  • No other animals or loud clippers next door
  • Familiar smells and people
  • Time to settle without a clock running

Fear is easiest to manage when we remove the things that cause it.

What we do

What we will never do

Lines we do not cross

We do not sedate. Sedation is a veterinary decision with real risks, and it has no place in a grooming session. If a pet truly cannot be groomed safely without it, that is a conversation with your vet, not us.

We do not scruff, hold down or tie a frightened pet, and we do not use a muzzle as a shortcut or a punishment. If your dog has a history of biting, tell us when you book and we will talk through what is safe before a groomer is sent.

We do not finish at all costs. If your pet has reached its limit, we stop. A groom that leaves your pet more afraid than before has failed, however tidy the coat looks.

A nervous pet is told, through our actions, that it has a say. That is the point.

Every life stage

Cats: closed room, quiet voice, no chase

A different kind of nervous

Cats do not tolerate being chased, held down or hurried. The groomer works in one closed room so a startled cat cannot bolt, and everything is done at a pace the cat accepts. A towel wrap is used for comfort and security, not for restraint.

Warning signs are quick and clear: flattened ears, a thumping tail, growling, dilated pupils. When they appear, the groomer pauses. Many cats settle after a break, and many do not, in which case we stop.

A cat that has had only a part of the grooming completed and ended calmly is a successful visit. The rest can wait.

  • One closed, quiet room
  • A towel for comfort, not force
  • Breaks whenever the cat shows stress
  • No chasing, scruffing or sedation

Festivals, fireworks and a Pune pet’s nerves

Why timing matters

Diwali, Ganeshotsav processions with dhol-tasha and monsoon thunder all push sensitive pets towards anxiety. A pet that is already unsettled is a poor candidate for a grooming session, and we will happily rebook.

In the days around loud festivals, aim for calm: keep to routine, create a quiet room and consider asking your vet for advice on noise phobia. Book grooming well before or after, not during.

A bath the day before a loud festival can make a nervous pet feel more settled, but only if the pet enjoys the bath. If not, skip it.

The best time to groom a nervous pet is when it is already having a calm day.

Helping a nervous pet get better, one visit at a time

Progress is real, but slow

Most nervous pets improve with repeated gentle exposure. The first visit may only manage a brush and a towel dry. The second adds the bath. By the third or fourth, many pets cope with the whole routine.

You can help by practising small things between visits: touching paws, running the dryer in another room, rewarding calm behaviour and keeping the groomer’s visit predictable. Short, frequent visits beat long, rare ones.

If your pet’s fear is severe, with panic, aggression or self-injury, talk to your vet or a qualified behaviourist as well. Grooming can be one part of the plan but it is not a substitute for treating anxiety.

Tell us honestly how your pet behaves. It helps us keep everyone safe and calm.

Anxious & nervous pets: frequently asked questions

What is Special Handling and how much is it?

Special Handling is a +₹199 add-on for dogs who need extra patience, time and sometimes extra hands: nervous, reactive, strong or senior dogs. It is optional and the groomer will only suggest it if it will genuinely help.

Do you sedate nervous pets?

No, never. Sedation is a veterinary decision with real risks and it has no place in grooming. If your pet cannot be groomed safely without it, please speak to your vet.

Do you use a muzzle?

Not as a shortcut and never as a punishment. If your dog has a history of biting, tell us when you book so we can discuss what is safe for everyone, including whether a vet or behaviourist should be involved first.

My dog bites. Can you still groom him?

Please tell us honestly when you book. For some dogs, a calm home setting and a patient approach works well; for others, a vet or behaviourist should be involved first. We will not take a risk with a pet’s or a groomer’s safety.

What if my pet will not let the groomer touch him?

Then we do not force it. We spend the time letting him settle, introduce things gradually and do whatever he allows. We will explain what was achieved and plan a shorter follow-up.

Can you groom a cat that hisses and scratches?

Often yes, with a quiet approach in a closed room and breaks. If the cat is too distressed, we stop. We do not sedate or restrain cats, and we tell you honestly what was possible.

Should I be there during the session?

Usually yes. Many nervous pets are calmer when their person is nearby. If your presence makes your pet more excited, the groomer may suggest you stay in another room. We follow what works for your pet.

How long does it take?

Longer than a normal visit. We plan for extra time and breaks, so please do not book a tight slot. The first visit may be a partial groom, and that is fine.

Will my nervous dog get better with time?

Most do, with repeated short, positive visits. Each calm experience builds confidence. If fear is severe, combine grooming with advice from your vet or a qualified behaviourist.

What should I do before the visit?

Tell us everything that frightens your pet, walk your dog beforehand so they are tired and calm, keep the home quiet and have a few of their favourite treats ready if your vet allows. Practising touching paws and ears in the days before helps.
